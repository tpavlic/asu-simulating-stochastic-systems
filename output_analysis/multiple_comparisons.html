This tab simulates the null hypothesis of one system design that meets all K of its performance requirements, and it estimates how often the design will be falsely reported as missing at least one of them. One design is simulated R times and judged on K responses (a mean wait, a utilization, a fill rate, and so on), each against its own target, and the report is one yes or no: does the design meet every requirement? Those K tests are a family because the report is wrong whenever any one of them is wrong. Every response's true mean sits exactly at its target, and so every flag is a false alarm; the fraction of runs in which the design is reported as missing a requirement is the family's Type-I error rate. Judge K requirements at level α each, and the chance that at least one verdict is wrong, called the family-wise error rate, climbs with K. This tab counts that rate directly, and the Bonferroni correction control shows what holding it at α does to every interval. Had the question been which of K separate designs miss a target, each judged and acted on alone, each verdict would carry its own α and no family correction would be wanted; a family matters only when one decision rests on all K tests, as it does here and wherever designs are compared with each other. The one-shot version of this tab is Hotelling's T², which judges all K requirements in one statistic; with several designs instead of one it becomes MANOVA, which stands to tab ⑤'s ANOVA as Hotelling's T² stands to this tab. Tab ②, Designs Against Each Other, compares designs with each other, against a benchmark or over every pair; tab ③, Power Under Correction, measures the power the correction gives up; and tab ⑤, ANOVA and Post Hoc Tests, answers whether any design differs with one F test and then which ones differ with a post hoc test.

Comparing designs means intervals on differences: a benchmark against every other design gives K − 1 of them, and every pair gives K(K − 1)/2. The intervals share data, and so the independent-comparisons formula is no longer exact, but the Bonferroni bound needs no independence at all, which is why it is the textbook's choice when designs are run with common random numbers.

A correction that makes false alarms rarer also makes true detections rarer. Plant a real difference and watch how often each rule finds it. Holm's step-down keeps the same family-wise guarantee as Bonferroni and gives some of the power back, and because it works on the sorted p-values rather than on the intervals, this is the tab where p-values get a column of their own. Holm's first threshold is Bonferroni's, and so it helps only once more than one comparison is real. At every pair among ten designs, 45 comparisons, each Bonferroni interval is about 1.8 times as wide as the uncorrected one, and only large differences are still detected; that loss is what motivates a one-shot test such as tab ⑤, ANOVA and Post Hoc Tests.

One-way ANOVA's logic runs in two steps: the F test asks whether any design differs, and a post hoc test asks which. The F test holds α without a correction, but it does not say which designs differ, and each post hoc test here is a single critical difference on the sorted means: Tukey's honestly significant difference from the studentized range, Fisher's least significant difference run only after a significant F, and Bonferroni's t at α/C. Bonferroni-corrected pairwise intervals (the ones tab ②, Designs Against Each Other, builds) are themselves a legitimate post hoc test, which is why they sit beside Tukey's and Fisher's here. With K responses in place of one, ANOVA becomes MANOVA, the way tab ①'s K one-sample intervals become Hotelling's T².

Tukey's honestly significant difference and Bonferroni's t both judge every pair at once, and the two critical-difference cards above show the gain is real but modest: Tukey's is the smaller of the two, never the larger, but rarely by much.

Fisher's protected LSD holds the family-wise error rate at α only when there are exactly three designs (Hayter, 1986); with five, and with the last two designs planted δ above the rest by default, F almost always flags a difference, and so the protection does nothing and the four null pairs (three among the unmoved designs and one between the two moved ones) are each tested at a bare α. Watch its family-wise error rate above settle well past α, whereas Tukey's and Bonferroni's do not.

ANOVA and all three post-hoc rules here assume every design is sampled independently of every other, an assumption common random numbers break by design: each rule pools a within-design variance across designs that no longer vary independently, Bonferroni's rule included. Under common random numbers, a comparison instead falls back to the paired interval at α/C that tab ② builds, where the Bonferroni inequality still holds because it needs no independence at all. When every comparison is against one benchmark design rather than among all of them, Dunnett's test is the exact many-versus-one counterpart of Tukey's rule here.

A fixed-sample test cannot add replications where they turn out to be needed. This two-stage procedure can. A pilot stage of R₀ replications per design screens out designs that are clearly worse, a second stage adds replications only to the survivors, as many as each one's variance and the practically significant difference ε call for, and the survivor with the best mean is selected. With probability at least 1 − α the selection is the best design or one within ε of it.

A fixed-sample test such as one-way ANOVA draws the same number of replications from every design no matter how the data turn out; it cannot add replications where they turn out to be needed. This procedure can: its second stage adds replications only to the survivors of screening and only as many as each survivor's own variance and ε call for.

Arena's Process Analyzer draws this same idea as a best-scenario chart: one box plot per design, with the best one marked.

A screening-only variant of the procedure stops after step three and selects directly among the survivors, using a t quantile at the upper-tail probability 1 − (1 − α)^(1/(K−1)) instead of splitting the guarantee between two stages. A selection-only variant skips screening altogether and takes every design to the same second-stage size using Rinott's constant h(R₀, K, 1 − α) because the whole guarantee then falls on that one stage.

The 1 − α guarantee above is proved for normal data sampled independently across designs, and the procedure is also used with common random numbers in practice.

Ranking and selection decides among a handful of named designs. When a design is a point in a parameter space, such as a staffing level, a reorder point, or a batch size, the candidates cannot be enumerated: the search itself decides where to simulate next and how much, and every estimate it compares carries noise.

The adaptive loop
What drives the search

Metaheuristics search by trial and improvement rather than by gradient. Scatter search paired with tabu memory is the engine inside OptQuest, which Arena, AnyLogic, and other simulation packages license. The other common families borrow their moves from nature, each keeping a population or a search history that steers the next round of candidates away from ground already covered:

  • Genetic algorithms: a population of encoded candidates evolves by selection, crossover, and mutation, and the building blocks of good candidates spread through it.
  • Evolution strategies, including CMA-ES: real-valued mutation whose step sizes and directions adapt to the shape of the objective as the search proceeds.
  • Differential evolution: each new candidate is built from scaled differences between existing population members, and so the population's own spread sets the step.
  • Simulated annealing: a single candidate takes random moves and accepts a worse one with a probability that falls as a temperature cools, climbing out of local optima early and settling later.
  • Particle swarm optimization: candidates move as particles pulled toward their own best position and the swarm's best, the way a flock turns together.
  • Ant colony optimization: for path-like or discrete designs, candidates are built step by step along pheromone trails that good solutions reinforce.
  • Tabu search: a local search that forbids recently made moves for a while; this is the memory OptQuest pairs with scatter search.
  • Cross-entropy method and estimation-of-distribution algorithms: a sampling distribution over the design space is refit to each round's best candidates, and the next round is drawn from it.

Driven by a simulation, every one of these evaluates a noisy objective, and so each has a version that re-evaluates its elites, averages replications before comparing, or hands its finalists to ranking and selection. A wider survey of metaheuristics lists many more.

The methods above treat the simulation model as a black box that can only be probed. An alternative approach is to build a meta-model of the simulation model that helps to identify generalizable structure across the parameter space. For example, response-surface and stochastic-approximation methods fit a local model of the objective and follow that model toward an optimum.

Ranking and selection fits into the loop in two places. Page ⑥'s two-stage procedure is the clean-up step, deciding among the search's finalists with a stated guarantee once the search itself has narrowed the field; a lighter screening rule can also sit inside the loop, stopping replications on a candidate that is already clearly worse than the others.

Two consequences of random simulation output

The search never sees a candidate's true expected performance. Every value it compares is a sample mean from a finite number of replications. The candidate with the largest sample mean is partly the candidate that happened to draw favorably, and so its sample mean overstates its true performance, as page ④ shows for a fixed set of designs. This is why the loop's last step re-estimates the chosen candidate on fresh replications before reporting it: a sample mean and interval computed from new replications are not biased by the selection that picked the candidate.

Every comparison the search makes is one more hypothesis test, and a search makes thousands of them. If the rule were simply to move to any candidate whose interval excludes the current best's at α = 0.05, then one comparison in twenty between two equally good candidates would move the search to the wrong one. Pages ①–③ measure exactly this accumulation of false alarms. The search rules above therefore do not decide each comparison once: they keep replicating candidates that are close and drop only candidates that are clearly worse.

How this differs from the rest of this widget

Pages ①–⑤ compare a fixed set of designs against fixed samples from each. Page ⑥ adds replications adaptively but still to a fixed set of designs chosen in advance. This page searches an open set: the design space itself is not enumerated, and the statistical tools built on the earlier pages (confidence intervals, corrections, and a screening rule) appear here again, now as components inside a search loop rather than as the whole procedure.
