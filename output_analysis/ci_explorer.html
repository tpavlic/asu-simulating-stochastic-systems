Each run draws n values from the normal population by inverse-transform sampling, computes x and S, and forms the interval x ± t·S/√n. The population is known here on purpose: it is the only way to check whether an interval did its job. Run many experiments, and watch the tally settle near α, the fraction that the confidence level promises will miss. Drag the dashed μ line to move the mean, or drag the curve itself to change σ; the fields above follow as you drag, and the last sample stays put so you can see the population move beneath it. The x-axis stays fixed so a change of σ shows as a change of width; μ can move only within it, and Clear history re-centers the axes on the current population.

Where the interval comes from and what changes its width
A one-sample t test at level α fails to reject the hypothesized mean μ whenever |x − μ| ≤ tα/2,n−1·S/√n. Solving that inequality for μ gives the set of means the data cannot rule out, which is the confidence interval. An interval that excludes a hypothesized value is the same thing as a rejection at level α.
Half-width:H = tα/2,n−1 · S / √n
Interval:[ x − H , x + H ]
Guarantee:Pr( μ ∈ [x − H, x + H] ) = 1 − α
Lower α: wider, misses lessRaising the confidence level raises t. The interval widens, and fewer of them miss. At 99.9% almost none miss; at 40% most do. This is the only control that changes the miss rate.
Larger n: narrower, same miss rateH shrinks like 1/√n, and t itself falls toward 1.96 as the degrees of freedom grow. Four times the samples halves the width. The fraction that miss stays at α.
Larger σ: wider, same miss rateH grows in proportion to the spread of the population because S estimates σ. Doubling σ doubles the width, and again the fraction that miss stays at α.

Both arms cost the same 2n simulation runs. In the independent arm each system draws its own input; in the common-random-numbers arm pair i feeds both systems the same input and the analysis moves to the n differences di = y1i − y2i. Input variance that both outputs share cancels in the difference, and so the paired interval is narrower by about √(1 − ρ) and more often excludes zero, which is the evidence that the systems differ.

Why sharing inputs narrows the interval and why the analysis must then be paired
Variance of the difference:Var(ȳ₁ − ȳ₂) = (σ₁² + σ₂² − 2ρσ₁σ₂) / n
With σ₁ = σ₂ = σ:= 2σ²(1 − ρ) / n
Independent inputs make ρ zero. Feeding both systems the same input makes their outputs move together, ρ > 0, and the shared part of the variance cancels in the difference. What remains reflects how the systems differ, not how the inputs happened to fall. The price is that the two samples are no longer independent, and the two-sample (Welch or pooled) interval no longer applies: the n differences are independent of each other, and the one-sample interval on them is the right tool. That is the paired-difference t test in its interval form.
Two-sample ruleAn interval for μ₁ − μ₂ that excludes zero is a rejection of "no difference" at level α. Comparing two separate one-sample intervals for overlap is a weaker and less reliable test.
Set ρ to zeroWith nothing shared, the paired interval is slightly wider than Welch's: it spends n − 1 degrees of freedom where Welch has about 2(n − 1). Pairing only pays when the inputs matter.
Set ρ near oneAlmost all of the variance is input, and the paired interval collapses onto the true difference. This is why a seed shared across designs is the first variance-reduction tool to reach for when comparing systems.
The sample-size side of the same fact, how many paired runs a given correlation saves, is on the Power Explorer's paired-runs tab.

Both arms cost 2n evaluations of the model and share the first n draws. The independent arm follows them with n fresh draws and averages all 2n values. The antithetic arm follows them with their mirrors 1 − u, averages each pair, and treats the n pair means as the sample. When h rises or falls steadily, a high draw is paired with a low one, the pair means cluster around the true mean, and the interval narrows.

Why negatively correlated pairs narrow the interval
Pair mean:Z = (Ya + Yb) / 2,  E[Z] = E[Y]
Its variance:Var(Z) = ( Var Ya + Var Yb + 2 Cov(Ya, Yb) ) / 4
With antithetic inputs:Ya = h(U),  Yb = h(1 − U)
Averaging two values costs nothing in bias, and the variance of the average falls below half the variance of one value exactly when the two are negatively correlated. Two independent draws never are. But U and 1 − U are perfectly negatively correlated, and if h is monotone (rising or falling throughout), a large h(U) comes with a small h(1 − U), and so the outputs inherit a negative correlation from the inputs. Each pair is one independent observation, and so the n pair means go through the ordinary interval formula, and the generator is only consulted n times for 2n model evaluations.
Pick the bowlFor h(u) = (u − ½)², the mirror 1 − u gives exactly the same value, and so the "pair mean" is the value itself. The antithetic interval is built from n values, whereas the independent one has 2n, and it comes out wider. Monotonicity is the whole trick.
The limiting caseA model that is a linear function of a normal inverse transform, h(u) = μ + σΦ⁻¹(u), has h(1 − u) = 2μ − h(u): every pair mean is exactly μ, and the interval has zero width. Real models sit between that and the bowl.
Antithetic and common random numbersBoth manipulate inputs to induce correlation. Common random numbers want positive correlation between two systems' outputs so their difference is stable; antithetic variates want negative correlation within one system so an average is stable.

The same n draws feed both arms. The plain arm averages y = h(u). The control-variate arm notices that u itself is available, has known mean ½, and co-varies with y, and so a run whose u values happened to come out high will have a y average that is high for that reason. Subtracting c(ū − ½) corrects for it. With c chosen to minimize the variance, the interval shrinks by the factor √(1 − r²).

Control-variate estimate z = h(u) − c(u − ½)
Why a correlated quantity with a known mean reduces variance
Adjusted variable:Z = Y − c (X − E[X]),  E[Z] = E[Y] for every c
Its variance:Var(Z) = Var Y − 2c Cov(X, Y) + c² Var X
Best c:c* = Cov(X, Y) / Var X,  Var(Z) = Var Y · (1 − ρ²XY)
Any c leaves the mean alone because the term being subtracted has mean zero. The variance is a parabola in c with its minimum at the regression slope of Y on X, and at that slope the fraction of Var Y that survives is 1 − ρ². The more strongly the control co-varies with the output, the larger the reduction. Here the control is the model's own input, but in a simulation it can be anything with a known mean that the output responds to: the average of the sampled service times, say, whose mean is a parameter of the input model.
Pick the bowlFor h(u) = (u − ½)², the output is symmetric in u, and Cov(U, h(U)) is exactly zero. The fitted slope wobbles around zero from run to run, and the interval does not shrink. A control has to co-vary with the output to help.
Estimating c costs a littleThe slope is fitted from the same run it corrects, which makes the interval slightly optimistic. At n = 50, the capture rate is about two points under nominal; at n = 10, it runs about ten points under, near 83% for a nominal 95%. Larger runs, or a c fixed from a pilot, remove the shortfall.
The same idea as regressionThe control-variate estimate is the fitted value of the regression of Y on X at X = E[X]. Two or more controls generalize to a multiple regression in the same way.

Both arms spend n draws. The direct arm counts how many standard normals land beyond T; at T = 5 that count is almost always zero, and an interval built from all zeros is the single point [0, 0], which is wrong every time. The weighted arm draws from a normal centered at θ instead, and so the tail is visited often. It multiplies each tail draw by the likelihood ratio W(y) = φ(y)/φ(y − θ), which undoes the bias exactly. Push θ well past T, and the weights become so uneven that the interval is no longer trustworthy; the effective sample size in the stats says when that has happened. The weighted values are ordinary independent observations, and the usual interval applies to them.

Why sampling from the wrong distribution, corrected by a weight, gives the right answer
Change of measure:EX[g(X)] = ∫ g(x) fX(x) dx = ∫ g(x) · [fX(x)/fY(x)] · fY(x) dx = EY[g(Y) W(Y)]
Here:g(x) = 1[x ≥ T],  fX = φ(x),  fY = φ(x − θ),  W(y) = exp(−θy + θ²/2)
Estimator:p̂ = (1/n) Σ 1[Yi ≥ T] W(Yi),  Yi ~ N(θ, 1)
The identity holds for any sampling density that is positive wherever the target is, and so the sampler is free to put its draws where the interesting outcomes are. The weight pays back exactly what the bias borrowed. The variance, though, depends on the choice. Weights near one across the tail give a tight interval, whereas a shift far past T makes the few draws nearest T carry weights thousands of times larger than the rest, the effective sample size collapses, and the interval widens again; pushed far enough, that same shift makes the interval unreliable, and it can even run below zero for a quantity that is a probability. Slide θ past T, and watch the effective sample size in the stats fall.
Direct sampling at T = 5Ten million standard normals would be expected to produce about three tail events; a thousand produce none, the sample variance is zero, and the interval is [0, 0]. Its miss tally is 100 of 100, which is not a violation of the guarantee so much as a warning that the t interval needs some variation to work with.
The same interval formulaNothing changes downstream: the weighted values are independent, their mean is p, and the one-sample t interval captures p about 100(1 − α)% of the time. Importance sampling is a change of what is averaged, not of how the average is analyzed.
Bias on purposeThe other three techniques leave the sampling distribution alone and reduce variance by correlation or by a correction term. The right distribution almost never produces the event of interest; this one deliberately samples from the wrong distribution and then corrects for it with the weight.
